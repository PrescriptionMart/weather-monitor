The rules in the tool, and what backs each one

1 · The floor is 36°F (2°C). Anything below is an excursion, not automatic discard. USP General Chapter <659> defines controlled cold temperature as 2–8°C (36–46°F) and allows excursions between 2° and 15°C (36–59°F) during storage, shipping and distribution, for no more than 24 hours, provided the mean kinetic temperature stays at or below 8°C and there are no excursions below 2°C or above 15°C. That is why the sheet's refrigerated floor is 36°F rather than 32°F, and why a reading under 36°F triggers a judgment call. Whether a sub-2°C, non-freezing exposure is acceptable is the manufacturer's call (see rule 6), which is what the tool's cold-exposure card asks you to weigh.
USP <659> Packaging and Storage Requirements [1]; USP <1079.2> on MKT [3] verify
2 · Product that has frozen → REPLACE, regardless of packs or weather. Freezing is the irreversible excursion for most refrigerated biologics and suspensions: ice formation and cryo-concentration drive protein unfolding and aggregation, and adjuvanted or suspension products lose potency permanently. FDA and the American Diabetes Association say frozen insulin must not be used even after thawing, and to inspect for clumps, frosting, crystals or particles. CDC's toolkit warns that freeze-damaged inactivated product "may not appear frozen," so appearance can confirm freezing but cannot rule it out. That is why the tool's product-frozen box overrides everything, and why the cold-exposure card weighs how cold, for how long, and porch timing alongside appearance.
FDA insulin storage [6]; ADA [7]; CDC Vaccine Storage & Handling Toolkit [4]; WHO Temperature Sensitivity of Vaccines [5]; freeze-induced aggregation literature [8]
3 · The allowance line comes from the manufacturer, and it is one-time and cumulative. Every refrigerated product has a labeled room-temperature allowance in its prescribing information or patient instructions (Humira 14 days at ≤77°F, Dupixent 14 days at ≤77°F, Stelara 30 days at ≤86°F, Repatha 30 days at ≤77°F, Aimovig 7 days at ≤77°F, Mounjaro 21 days at ≤86°F, and so on). Most labels say the clock does not reset if the product goes back in the fridge and many say "do not return to the refrigerator." A 2025 review of every FDA-approved unopened refrigerated product found nearly 60% tolerate one week to one month at room temperature, and concluded that brand-specific manufacturer data must be the basis for excursion decisions. Practice guidance (NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service; U.S. Pharmacist) describes the same process the tool follows: gather time, temperature and first-exposure facts, compare against the manufacturer's data, and request a stability letter when the label is silent.
Aleidi et al., Cureus 2025 [9]; U.S. Pharmacist 2022 [10]; NHS SPS, Managing temperature excursions [11]; manufacturer prescribing information [12]
4 · The pack-out is rated for 48 hours, so days inside that window are "covered." Insulated shippers with gel packs are qualified against standard ambient profiles for a stated duration. ISTA 7D provides steady-state summer and winter profiles in 24-, 48- and 72-hour lengths; ISTA 7E (Standard 20) provides diurnal summer and winter profiles built from real US parcel lanes, up to 72 hours and repeatable to 144; ASTM D3103 is the insulation-performance test method. USP <1079> expects shipping systems to be qualified under expected seasonal extremes with the actual pack-out, and URAC's Medication Distribution standards (P-MD) require annual qualification testing with continuous monitors and a written definition of what counts as an excursion for each temperature profile. "Qualified for 48 hours" is the standard vendor and lab phrasing. The tool's PACKOUT_RATING_HOURS constant is that number.
ISTA 7D [13]; ISTA 7E / Standard 20 [14]; ASTM D3103-20 [15]; USP <1079> [2]; URAC P-MD [16]; ACHC cold-chain guidance [17]
5 · Outdoor air at the nearest NWS station is a worst-case proxy, with a van-in-the-sun caveat. When there is no data logger in the box, cold-chain investigation guidance lists local weather data as supporting evidence. A 2023 study in the Journal of the American Pharmacists Association shipped logger packages by three carriers in winter and summer and found packages spent most of transit time outside 20–25°C and hit both extremes; it deliberately stopped measuring at delivery, so porch time is unstudied. ISTA's parcel-lane data behind 7E found readings below 0°C or above 35°C rare and short-lived, which supports treating outdoor air as a reasonable worst case for the box's surroundings. The exception is vehicle interiors: 2024 reporting on mail-order medications quoted drivers describing 150°F cargo areas, and parked-vehicle studies show cabins running 20°C above ambient in sun. That is why the heat card says outdoor air is the worst case for the porch but a van can run hotter.
Chowdhury et al., JAPhA 2023 [18]; ISTA 7E lane data [14]; NYT / Becker's 2024 [19]; parked-vehicle cabin study [20]
6 · A JUDGMENT CALL means: weigh the factors, document, and call the manufacturer when it is close. CDC's excursion procedure is the template used across pharmacy: label the product "DO NOT USE," separate it, document date, time and temperatures, and contact the manufacturer for a viability decision based on their stability data. The tool's Copy summary is the documentation step; the keep-it and replace-it scripts are the two outcomes. Texas rule 22 TAC §291.12 (adopted 2024) requires common-carrier deliveries to be packaged so the drug stays within the USP or manufacturer temperature range until the delivery has been received by the patient or patient's agent, and lets the dispensing pharmacist refuse common-carrier delivery when it could clinically compromise the drug. Receipt by the patient is the regulatory boundary, which is why the tool scores delayed days but does not hold the pharmacy to overnight porch dwell.
CDC, Handling a Temperature Excursion [4]; 22 TAC §291.12 [21]; NHS SPS [11]
7 · Appearance is one factor, not proof. Visual inspection (clarity, color, particles, ice crystals) is part of every excursion procedure, but two limits apply. Freeze damage is often invisible (rule 2). And heat degradation usually produces no visible change either; 2024 reporting documented patients being told a product was fine because it "looked normal." The WHO shake test detects freeze damage only in aluminium-adsorbed vaccines and is not applicable to the products on this list. So the tool pairs appearance with exposure-versus-allowance and pack state rather than letting appearance decide alone.
CDC Toolkit [4]; Kartoglu et al., Bull WHO 2010 [22]; NYT / Becker's 2024 [19]

Where the literature is thin or disagrees

  • Sub-2°C but not frozen. USP <659> permits no excursion below 2°C, yet manufacturer letters routinely clear brief mild cold exposure of a normal-looking product. The tool treats it as a judgment call, not a discard, which matches practice rather than the letter of the chapter.
  • MKT is not a rescue for cold. USP <1079.2> allows MKT for evaluating short heat excursions, not repeated ones, and it is not appropriate for freeze events or for proteins whose degradation is not Arrhenius-like. The tool does not compute MKT for that reason. verify
  • Porch exposure is unstudied. No published study instruments refrigerated parcels after delivery. The 48-hour coverage and the "few hours of dwell" posture come from the pack-out rating and 22 TAC §291.12's receipt boundary, not from porch measurements.
  • Vehicle temperatures are anecdotal. The 150°F figure is driver-reported, not instrumented. Treat "van in the sun" as a reason to lean replace on a hot day, not as a number.
  • Enbrel. The sheet lists 30 days for all formulations; the manufacturer gives 30 days for the prefilled syringe and SureClick but 14 days for the multi-dose vial and dose tray. Worth confirming in the spreadsheet. verify
  • ISTA 7E extremes. The exact profile peaks and minimums are inside the paywalled standard and could not be confirmed; only the "rare above 35°C / below 0°C" characterization is public.

Wording the pharmacy can cite in an SOP

  • Refrigerated products are stored at 2–8°C (36–46°F). Per USP <659>, excursions between 2°C and 15°C not exceeding 24 hours are permitted in distribution; any reading below 2°C or above 15°C is evaluated as an excursion.
  • Shipping containers are qualified for 48 hours against ISTA 7D/7E summer and winter profiles, consistent with USP <1079> and URAC Medication Distribution standards, and re-qualified at least annually.
  • A suspected excursion is evaluated against the manufacturer's labeled room-temperature allowance and stability data (per manufacturer prescribing information and published reviews), with the product quarantined and the event documented per the CDC excursion procedure; the manufacturer is contacted when the label is silent or the exposure approaches the allowance.
  • Product showing evidence of freezing is not dispensed or used, regardless of appearance after thawing (FDA/ADA insulin guidance; CDC).
  • Per 22 TAC §291.12, common-carrier shipments are packaged to maintain USP or manufacturer temperature range until receipt by the patient or agent; the pharmacist may refuse common-carrier delivery when it would clinically compromise the product.
